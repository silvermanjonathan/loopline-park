Chunks explored
Chunk 1 · Your first line — then break itE5-PRO-TR-?? ⚠

The computer does not guess

Type this one line into your file, exactly as written, and run it.

print("Welcome to Loopline Park!")
Predict What appears in the output panel — and will the quotation marks be part of it? Say your guess out loud, then run.
The words appear without the quotation marks. The quotes are for Python, not for the guests — they mark where your message starts and ends. Everything between them is called a string. Now sabotage your own line: change print to pint and run again. You get an error message called a traceback: 
Traceback (most recent call last):
  File "day1.py", line 1, in <module>
    pint("Welcome to Loopline Park!")
NameError: name 'pint' is not defined

Engineer's rule: read the traceback last line first. It names the problem (pint is not a thing) and the line number where it lives. The computer does only what is written — nothing has ever demanded exactness from you like this before, and that's the whole game.

You should see 
Welcome to Loopline Park!
…and then, after the sabotage, the traceback. Fix it back before Chunk 2.
Chunk 2 · The marqueeE5-PRO-PD-?? ⚠

Build the entrance marquee

One print() makes one line of output. Stack several to build the big sign — and label the sections with #. A line starting with # is a comment: a note for human engineers that Python skips completely.

# Top border
print("* * * * * * * * * * *")
# Park name
print("   LOOPLINE  PARK")
print("  GATES OPEN TODAY")
# Bottom border
print("* * * * * * * * * * *")
Predict How many lines of output will this program make — four, five, or six?
Four. There are six lines of code, but two are comments, and comments never print. Each print() makes exactly one output line, in the order written — top to bottom, like reading. Real engineers label their work so the next person (or Friday-you) knows what each part does.
You should see 
* * * * * * * * * * *
   LOOPLINE  PARK
  GATES OPEN TODAY
* * * * * * * * * * *
Now make it yours: change the words, add a line, add more border rows.
Chunk 3 · VariablesE5-PRO-VD-?? ⚠

A labeled box that holds a value

The ticket booth needs to remember the price. In Python, = does not mean "equals." It means "gets the value of." Say it out loud: "price gets 20."

price = 20
print(price)
price = 15
print(price)
Predict What two numbers print? And after line 3 runs, is price both 20 and 15?
It prints 20, then 15. A variable is a labeled box holding one value at a time. Line 3 takes the 20 out and puts 15 in — the 20 is gone. That's why = is an action, not a fact. Fill in the first two rows of your trace table for this program: one row per line, tracking what price holds.

20
15
Chunk 4 · Listening to guests

input() — the booth asks a question

name = input("Welcome! What is your name? ")
print("Hello, " + name + "!")
Predict When you run this, what happens first — does anything print right away? What is the program waiting for?
The question prints, then the program stops and waits. Whatever the guest types gets stored in the box labeled name. The + glues strings together, so the greeting uses whatever name was typed.

Chunk 5 · A famous bugE5-PRO-TR-?? ⚠

The '2' + '3' problem

The booth needs to add two numbers a guest types. Try it the obvious way:

a = input("First number? ")
b = input("Second number? ")
print(a + b)
Predict Type 2 and then 3. What will print — 5, or something else?
It prints 23. Here's the rule that explains it: input() always hands back text, even when the guest types digits. So a holds the string "2" and b holds the string "3", and + glues strings together instead of adding. It looks numeric on screen — that's exactly why this bug fools professional engineers too. Try print(type(a)) to see the proof: <class 'str'>.
Chunk 6 · int() at the doorE5-PRO-PD-?? ⚠

Convert, then compute the ticket price

Fix the bug by converting text to a number the moment it arrives — "convert at the door." Then use an f-string to build a tidy receipt: put an f before the quotes, and anything inside { } gets swapped for its value. Keep this tool close: on Wednesday, three f-strings exactly like this one paint a live roller-coaster dashboard sixty times a second.

name = input("Welcome! What is your name? ")
tickets = int(input("How many tickets? "))
total = tickets * 12
print(f"{name}, {tickets} tickets cost ${total}.")
Predict If the guest is Sam and buys 3 tickets, what exact line prints? Trace it: what does total hold before line 4 runs?
total holds 36 (3 × 12), so it prints Sam, 3 tickets cost $36. The int() wrapper turns the typed "3" into the number 3 before it ever lands in the box — so the math works. Finish your trace table for this program: four lines, four rows.
You should see 
Welcome! What is your name? Sam
How many tickets? 3
Sam, 3 tickets cost $36.
Chunk 7 · The drafting turtle, the long way

A square track, eight lines of code

New file: day1_track.py. The turtle is your drafting pen — it starts in the middle of a window, facing right, and draws wherever it walks. forward(100) walks 100 steps; right(90) turns 90 degrees clockwise on the spot. The park wants a square child-sized coaster track, and here's the brute-force blueprint:

import turtle
t = turtle.Turtle()

t.forward(100)
t.right(90)
t.forward(100)
t.right(90)
t.forward(100)
t.right(90)
t.forward(100)
t.right(90)

turtle.done()
Predict Look at the eight drawing lines. How many of them are copies of each other? What exact pair repeats, and how many times?
All eight are copies: the pair forward(100) + right(90), repeated 4 times. Imagine a 36-sided track this way — 72 lines. Repeating yourself is the computer's job, not yours. That's what the next chunk fixes. (turtle.done() at the end keeps the window open — it goes at the bottom of every drawing file.)
You should see A window opens and a square gets drawn one side at a time, ending back where the turtle started.
Chunk 8 · The for loopE5-ALG-PS-01

Same square, three lines

import turtle
t = turtle.Turtle()

for i in range(4):
    t.forward(100)
    t.right(90)

turtle.done()
Predict The loop body — the indented lines — is written once. How many times does it actually run: once, or four times? What will the drawing look like?
Four times — the exact same square as Chunk 7. for i in range(4): means "run the indented block 4 times." Written once ≠ runs once: that's the single most important idea of the afternoon — and on Wednesday you'll watch four lines written once run over a thousand times to fly a roller coaster. Prove it to yourself now: add print("lap!") inside the loop and count the laps in the output panel.
You should see The identical square from Chunk 7 — from 8 drawing lines down to 3. If you added the print, the word lap! appears 4 times.
Chunk 9 · The angle ruleE5-PRO-PD-?? ⚠

360 ÷ sides — a pentagon track

Change two numbers and the square becomes any shape. But which turn angle? To finish a lap, the turtle's turns must add up to one full spin: 360°.

for i in range(5):
    t.forward(100)
    t.right(72)
Predict — this one traps almost everyone Geometry class says a pentagon's interior angle is 108°. So should line 3 say right(108) or right(72)? Why?
72, because 360 ÷ 5 = 72. The turtle turns through the exterior angle — walk a pentagon on the floor and you'll feel it: at each corner you swing past the straight-ahead direction by 72°, and five of those swings make one full 360° spin. Try right(108) anyway and watch the drawing go wrong — a star-ish tangle, not a track. And push the rule: range(36) with forward(10) and right(10) (36 × 10 = 360, still one full spin) draws something that looks like a circle — the loop-the-loop blueprint, 72 brute-force lines for the price of 3.

Hinge question · stop here until you're sureE5-PRO-VD-?? ⚠

The two-price ticket, and the octagon contract

Two quick ones — commit to both before you tap. First: a program runs price = 20 and then price = 15. What does price hold now — 20, 15, both, or 35? Second: the park orders an octagon track, 8 sides. What angle goes in t.right( ? )?

15, and only 15 — a variable holds one value, and = replaces it. If you said "both" or "20," re-read Chunk 3 before the build; everything for the rest of camp depends on this. And 45, because 360 ÷ 8 = 45 — if you reached for the interior angle (135), walk the shape once more. Two rights? You're cleared for the build.
Build brief · The Booth & the Track

Open the gates, then draw the midway

Two small builds, in order. Part 1 — The Ticket Booth (in day1.py):

1. Prints your marquee, with a comment labeling each section.
2. Asks the guest's name with input().
3. Asks how many tickets they want, converting with int().
4. Computes the total (tickets cost $12) and prints a receipt with an f-string.

Part 2 — The Track Blueprint (in day1_track.py): a polygon coaster track of your shape — you pick the sides, you compute the angle with 360 ÷ sides, the loop does the repeating. Add t.color("red") before drawing to paint it. Nobody else's track should match yours.

Useful pieces: print() · input() · int() · * · f"..." · # comments · for i in range(n): · t.forward() / t.right()

Suggested start: get the marquee printing first, then add one question at a time, running after every line. For the track: run the square as-is, then change only the sides count and its matching angle. One change per run — that's how engineers keep control of a blueprint.

# --- Marquee ---
print("* * * * * * * * *")
print("  LOOPLINE PARK")
print("* * * * * * * * *")

# --- Ticket booth ---
name = input("Welcome! What is your name? ")
tickets = int(input("How many tickets? "))
total = tickets * 12
print(f"{name}, {tickets} tickets cost ${total}.")
# --- Track blueprint: a hexagon midway loop ---
import turtle
t = turtle.Turtle()
t.color("red")

for i in range(6):
    t.forward(90)
    t.right(60)

turtle.done()
Hexagon: 360 ÷ 6 = 60. Be ready to say your angle rule out loud at the gallery — and to name what = means in the booth.
