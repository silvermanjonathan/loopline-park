Chunks explored
Chunk 1 · Meet the drafting turtle

forward and right

The turtle is your drafting pen. It starts in the middle of the window, facing right, and draws wherever it walks.

import turtle
t = turtle.Turtle()

t.forward(100)
t.right(90)
t.forward(100)

turtle.done()
Predict right(90) turns the turtle 90 degrees clockwise on the spot — it doesn't move it. So what does this draw: an L, a T, or a straight line?
An L on its side: walk right 100 steps, turn to face down, walk 100 more. Turning and walking are separate moves. (turtle.done() at the end keeps the window open — desktop Python needs it, so it goes at the bottom of every drawing today.)
You should see A window opens and the turtle draws a horizontal line, then a vertical line down from its end — a corner.
Chunk 2 · The long way

A square track, eight lines of code

The park wants a square kiddie-coaster track. Here's the brute-force blueprint:

import turtle
t = turtle.Turtle()

t.forward(100)
t.right(90)
t.forward(100)
t.right(90)
t.forward(100)
t.right(90)
t.forward(100)
t.right(90)

turtle.done()
Predict Look at the eight drawing lines. How many of them are copies of each other? What exact pair repeats, and how many times?
All eight are copies: the pair forward(100) + right(90), repeated 4 times. Imagine a 36-sided track this way — 72 lines. Repeating yourself is the computer's job, not yours. That's what Day 2 fixes.
You should see A square, drawn one side at a time, ending back where the turtle started.
Chunk 3 · The for loopE5-ALG-PS-01

Same square, three lines

import turtle
t = turtle.Turtle()

for i in range(4):
    t.forward(100)
    t.right(90)

turtle.done()
Predict The loop body — the indented lines — is written once. How many times does it actually run: once, or four times? What will the drawing look like?
Four times — the exact same square as Chunk 2. for i in range(4): means "run the indented block 4 times." Written once ≠ runs once: that's the single most important idea today. Prove it to yourself — add print("lap!") inside the loop and count the laps in the output panel.
You should see The identical square from Chunk 2 — from 8 drawing lines down to 3. If you added the print, the word lap! appears 4 times.
Chunk 4 · The angle ruleE5-PRO-PD-?? ⚠

360 ÷ sides — a pentagon track

Change two numbers and the square becomes any shape. But which turn angle? To finish a lap, the turtle's turns must add up to one full spin: 360°.

for i in range(5):
    t.forward(100)
    t.right(72)
Predict — this one traps almost everyone Geometry class says a pentagon's interior angle is 108°. So should line 3 say right(108) or right(72)? Why?
72, because 360 ÷ 5 = 72. The turtle turns through the exterior angle — walk a pentagon on the floor and you'll feel it: at each corner you swing past the straight-ahead direction by 72°, and five of those swings make one full 360° spin. Try right(108) anyway and watch the drawing go wrong — a star-ish tangle, not a track.

Chunk 5 · Pushing the rule

The near-circle loop

for i in range(36):
    t.forward(10)
    t.right(10)
Predict 36 tiny sides, 10° turns (and 36 × 10 = 360 — the rule still holds). What shape comes out?
Something that looks like a circle — really a 36-sided polygon with sides too short to notice. This is the loop-the-loop blueprint. And notice: in Chunk 2 this drawing would have cost 72 lines of code. The loop costs 3.
You should see A small circle-looking track, drawn quickly side by side by side.
Chunk 6 · A loop inside a loopE5-ALG-PS-01

The Ferris-wheel rosette

One loop draws a square. What if a loop's body contains another whole loop? Draw a square, rotate a little, draw another square, rotate — twelve times around.

import turtle
t = turtle.Turtle()
t.speed(0)

for spoke in range(12):
    for side in range(4):
        t.forward(80)
        t.right(90)
    t.right(30)

turtle.done()
Predict The inner loop draws 4 sides. The outer loop runs 12 times. In total, how many times does t.forward(80) run? And why is the rotation 30°?
48 times — 4 sides × 12 spokes. Each pass of the outer loop runs the entire inner loop, then tilts the turtle 30° (because 360 ÷ 12 spokes = 30 — the same rule, now aimed at the wheel instead of the shape). Two loop variables, two jobs: side counts sides within one square, spoke counts squares around the wheel.
You should see Twelve overlapping squares fanned into a wheel — the Ferris-wheel rosette spinning at the top of this page. t.speed(0) makes the turtle draw at top speed so you're not waiting all afternoon.
Hinge question · stop here until you're sureE5-ALG-PS-01

The octagon contract

The park orders an octagon track — 8 sides. What angle goes in t.right( ? ), and how did you get it?

45, because 360 ÷ 8 = 45. If you reached for the interior angle (135) or guessed, walk the shape once more before the build — every blueprint you design next depends on this one rule.
Build brief · Design your wheel

Your own Ferris-wheel blueprint

Start from the Chunk 6 rosette and make a wheel nobody else in the room has. You control:

The spoke shape — square? pentagon? near-circle?  The spoke count — change range(12) and remember the rotation must be 360 ÷ your count.  The size — the forward() distance.  Color — t.color("blue") before drawing.

Suggested start: change only the spoke count first (and its matching angle). Run. Then change the shape. Run. One change per run — that's how engineers keep control of a blueprint.

import turtle
t = turtle.Turtle()
t.speed(0)
t.color("purple")

for spoke in range(10):
    for side in range(5):
        t.forward(70)
        t.right(72)
    t.right(36)

turtle.done()
Ten pentagon spokes: inner turn 360÷5 = 72, outer rotation 360÷10 = 36. Be ready to name your two loop variables and say what each one controls — that's the question at the blueprint gallery.

Choose your exit gate

Floor · approaching the standard

Middle · meets the standard

Stretch · exceeds the standard
