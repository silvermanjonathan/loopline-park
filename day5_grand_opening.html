Chunks explored
Chunk 1 · Rules vs. examples

Two ways to teach a machine

All week you wrote rules: if height < 48, family rides. You told the computer exactly what to do, and it did exactly that. But nobody ever gave you a rule for recognizing your best friend's face — you learned from examples.

Predict Could you write an if/elif/else rule that recognizes a thumbs-up in a photo? Try to imagine the conditions you'd need. What makes it so much harder than the height gate?
There's no clean rule to write — thumbs come at every angle, size, lighting, and skin tone, and "the thumb pixel is up" isn't a condition Python has. For problems like this, engineers flip the approach: instead of writing rules, they show the computer labeled examples and let it find its own patterns. That's machine learning — and it's what the park's new turnstile runs on.
Chunk 2 · Train the Smart TurnstileE5-ALG-ML-02

Teach it with your own examples

In the no-code tool, make two classes and train the park's turnstile:

1. Class one: OPEN — record about 15 thumbs-up photos of yourself. Vary it: near, far, left hand, right hand.
2. Class two: STAY CLOSED — record about 15 thumbs-down photos, varied the same way.
3. Train the model, then test it live.

Predict Now show it a brand-new thumbs-up it has never seen. Will it get it right — and if it does, does that mean it knows what a thumbs-up is?
It very likely says OPEN — but it doesn't know anything. It found patterns in your 15 examples and is matching the new photo against them. Every answer it ever gives traces back to an example somebody supplied. Keep that sentence; you'll need it in Chunk 3.
You should see A confidence bar (something like "OPEN — 97%") that updates live as you move your hand.
Chunk 3 · Break it on purposeE5-ALG-ML-02

The lopsided turnstile

Time for the Day-1 move, machine-learning edition: sabotage. Retrain the OPEN class using only your right hand, only close-up, only bright light — a deliberately lopsided set. Then test with your left hand, far away, in shade.

Predict Two predictions: will the model still act confident? And will it still be right?
Confidence stays high — the bar still says 90-something percent — while accuracy falls apart. The model can't tell you what it never saw; it just matches against a lopsided memory with total confidence. Now the park question: if this turnstile were real, whose examples were missing? Who would this gate fail? Guests with different hands, heights, skin tones, lighting... A gap in the training data becomes a gap in who gets through the gate. That is why real engineers ask "who is missing from my examples?" before they ship — it's a design choice about who the technology serves.
You should see High confidence, wrong answers — the most important broken thing you'll build all week.
Chunk 4 · The blueprintE5-ALG-PS-01

Plan the attraction before you type

Every ride at Loopline started as boxes and arrows. Your attraction does too: a visual plan showing the sequence (arrows), at least one loop (dashed box), at least one decision (red box), and the variables it uses. Here's a sample blueprint for a "Ride Recommender" booth:

START: greet guest, ask name
↓
LOOP: for each ride in rides → print it
↓
ask mood — "thrill or chill?"
↓
DECISION: mood == "thrill" ? → Comet : Tea Cups
↓
print the guest's ticket
Predict Which box is the decision, and which is the loop? And a check on the plan itself: which prior camp days do those two boxes come from?
The red box is the decision (Day 3), the dashed box is the loop (Day 2), and the name/mood questions are Day 1's input(). That's three days in one plan — the minimum for your own. Draw your blueprint on the template now. No code until your blueprint is approved and signed — that's the studio rule, and the cap is firm: one screen, one loop, one decision.
Chunk 5 · The debugging protocolE5-PRO-TR-?? ⚠

Read the error first

During the build, things will break. That's the plan working. This crash comes from a real Loopline machine — the roller-coaster simulator going home with every engineer today. Line 1 of the file says import math. Deep inside, one line reads:

        speed = sqrt(2 * GRAVITY * drop)
Traceback (most recent call last):
  File "coaster.py", line 71, in <module>
    speed = sqrt(2 * GRAVITY * drop)
NameError: name 'sqrt' is not defined
Predict Which line of the traceback do you read first? import math is right there at the top of the file — so what does Python not recognize, and what's the fix?
Last line first — the week's oldest rule. NameError: the same species as Monday's pint. Importing math carries the toolbox into the room, but you still have to name the drawer — the function's full name is math.sqrt, and on its own sqrt names nothing. The computer does not guess: day one's rule, still the whole game on day five. Fix: speed = math.sqrt(2 * GRAVITY * drop). (Today's likeliest crash in your own build is humbler — a missing : on an if line, SyntaxError — same protocol, last line first.) During the build, keep your two-line debugging log for every crash: what broke, what fixed it. Nobody fixes your code for you today — the log is proof it was you.
Hinge question · stop here until you're sureE5-ALG-ML-02

The wrong prediction

Your lopsided turnstile confidently called a real thumbs-up STAY CLOSED. In one sentence: why did the model get it wrong?

Because nothing like that photo was in its training examples — the model only matches against what it was shown, so a gap in the examples becomes a wrong answer. If your sentence says the model "didn't know" or "got confused," sharpen it: it's not about knowing. It's about what was missing from the examples. Write your final version on the reflection slip — it's collected.
Build brief · Your attraction

The Grand Opening build

Build the attraction your approved blueprint describes. The contract:

One screen. One loop. One decision. Constructs from at least three prior days. It runs, it matches the blueprint, and at the Grand Opening you present it in two minutes and name one design choice you made.

Your toolbox from the week: print() / input() / int() / f-strings (Day 1) · for + range(), turtle (Day 2) · if / elif / else, lists (Day 3) · random.choice(), def (Day 4).

Suggested start: build your blueprint's first box only. Run it. Then the next box. Run after every box — small steps leave small bugs.

# THE RIDE RECOMMENDER — Loopline Park
import random

rides = ["The Comet", "Splash Canyon", "Tea Cups", "Sky Drop"]

name = input("Welcome to Loopline Park! Your name? ")
print(f"Hello, {name}! Today's rides:")

for ride in rides:
    print("- " + ride)

mood = input("Feeling thrill or chill today? ")

if mood == "thrill":
    print(f"{name}, sprint to {rides[0]}!")
elif mood == "chill":
    print(f"{name}, drift over to {rides[2]}.")
else:
    print("Spin the wheel: " + random.choice(rides))
Day 1 (input, f-strings) + Day 2 (loop) + Day 3 (list, decision) + Day 4 (random.choice) — four days in 16 lines. Note the design choice in the else: a guest who types something unexpected gets the prize wheel instead of a crash. That's the kind of choice to name at your walkthrough.

Choose your exit gate

Floor · approaching the standard

Your blueprint is approved, and your program runs with a sequence plus a loop or a decision. You can point to each on your blueprint.

Middle · meets the standard

Your approved blueprint shows sequence, a loop, and a decision with its variables — and your program follows it, using constructs from at least three days. You present it and name one design choice.

Stretch · exceeds the standard

Your attraction survives a guest who types something unexpected without crashing (like the sample's else safety net) — and at the walkthrough you name the alternative design you rejected, and why.
