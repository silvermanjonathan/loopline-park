Chunks explored
Chunk 1 · The opening-day inspection

Run it once, exactly as it will run at 3:25

Every real park does a final inspection the morning of opening day. Run your build once, start to finish, and check three things: the ride completes (no banner), the booth survives a strange answer at its question, and you can say the three physics lines without looking. Anything fails → the clinic corner is open all morning — the clinic never really closes — while everyone else rehearses.

Predict Before the run: which of the three checks are you least sure of — and what's your plan if it fails?
Whatever you named — that's the one to rehearse hardest. A failed check this morning costs ten minutes; a failed check at the microphone costs the show. Real engineers inspect because they expect to find something.
Chunk 2 · The rehearsalE5-PRO-RD-?? ⚠

Run it once for one person

Before the room, one partner. Run your booth with them as the guest, and practice the narration: as your train runs, say the three physics lines on your own track — where-am-I (the height line), height-into-speed (the sqrt line), move-by-my-speed (the position line). Time it: the whole walkthrough is two minutes.

Predict Your partner will ask the hardest question in the park: "why does it speed up at the bottom?" Say your answer out loud now — without using the words "the code tells it to."
Something like: "Nothing tells it to speed up. The lower the train gets, the bigger the speed the sqrt line computes, so the position line takes bigger steps — the rushing happens on its own." If you can say that sentence, you understand your machine better than most adults who ride one. Last step of rehearsal: draft your reflection slip — one design choice you made, and who your ride serves… and who it doesn't.
3:25 · The Grand OpeningE5-ALG-IM-03

Two minutes at the microphone

Every engineer presents. The walkthrough contract:

1. Run your booth live, with a classmate as the guest — their answers, not yours.
2. As your ride runs, narrate the three physics lines on your own track.
3. Name one design choice you made, and who your ride serves — and who it doesn't. The 44-mph cruiser is for somebody. So is the 70-mph plunge. Neither is for everybody, and you chose. Hand in the reflection slip.

Then the closing conversation, led live: your program asks guests for their names. Who can see what they type? Who could change your track while you weren't looking? Who can reach this program at all? Real parks answer those three questions before opening day — and now, so have you.

After the ribbon One last question for the walk out, no reveal needed: of everything you fixed this week — the pint, the '2' + '3', the silent stamp, the stalled hill, today's bug — which fix taught you the most, and why?
